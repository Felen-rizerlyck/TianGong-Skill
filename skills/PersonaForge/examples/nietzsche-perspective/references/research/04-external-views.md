# External Views, Criticism and Analysis of Nietzsche

## Overview

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900) generated one of the most contentious and multifaceted philosophical legacies in Western thought. His reception history encompasses diametrically opposed interpretations, from being claimed as a prophet of Nazism to being celebrated as a liberator of postmodern thought. This document surveys the major critical works, controversies, contemporary reception, philosophical influences, relationships with other thinkers, and the notorious misuse of his ideas.

---

## I. Major Critical Works on Nietzsche

### A. Marxist Critiques

#### 1. Georg Lukács - The Destruction of Reason (1952)

**Source**: Georg Lukács, *The Destruction of Reason* (London: Merlin Press), originally published 1952-1954.

**Credibility**: Primary philosophical text from a major Marxist theorist; highly influential in communist criticism of Nietzsche.

**Summary**: Lukács provides an extensive Marxist critique of Nietzsche as the founder of irrationalism in the imperialist period. He argues that Nietzsche's philosophy represents a counter-Enlightenment reaction that:

- Parodies class struggle through the "struggle of masters and herd"
- Uses aristocratic epistemology to delegitimize socialist historicity
- Provides intellectual justification for fascism through irrationalist metaphysics

**Key Thesis**: Nietzsche inaugurated the form of irrationalism suited to monopoly capitalism, fusing anti-socialist polemic with cultural criticism that proved seductive for imperialist intellectuals.

**URL**: https://www.marxists.org/archive/lukacs/works/destruction-reason/ch03.htm

**Assessment**: While influential, Lukács's critique has been criticized for its political rigidity and failure to engage with the nuances of Nietzsche's actual texts. His reading of Nietzsche as primarily a political ideologue remains contested.

---

#### 2. Domenico Losurdo - Nietzsche, the Aristocratic Rebel (2017)

**Source**: Domenico Losurdo, *Nietzsche, the Aristocratic Rebel: Intellectual Biography and Critical Assessment* (Brill, 2017).

**Credibility**: Major scholarly work from Italian philosopher; highly detailed historical analysis.

**Summary**: Losurdo examines Nietzsche's philosophy in the context of 19th-century European politics, arguing that Nietzsche's aristocratic radicalism was a coherent political position with specific historical meanings that differ significantly from 20th-century fascism, though anticipating certain aspects of it.

**URL**: https://jacobin.com/2023/09/georg-lukacs-irrationalism-right-wing-thought-philosophy

**Assessment**: Provides important nuance between Nietzsche's actual views and later fascist appropriations while acknowledging the troubling elements in his thought.

---

### B. Heidegger's Nietzsche Interpretations

#### Martin Heidegger - Nietzsche Lectures (1936-1941, published 1961)

**Source**: Martin Heidegger, *Nietzsche* (4 volumes), originally lecture courses at Freiburg University 1936-1941, published 1961.

**Credibility**: Primary philosophical interpretation from one of the 20th century's most influential philosophers.

**Summary**: Heidegger interprets Nietzsche as the culmination and completion of Western metaphysics, not its destroyer:

- Views will to power as the fundamental ontological principle of all beings
- Sees eternal recurrence as a metaphysical vision of circular time
- Interprets "God is dead" as the death of the suprasensory world
- Positions Nietzsche within the history of Being

**Key Insight**: For Heidegger, Nietzsche is the "most thoughtful" thinker who "saw furthest" into the nihilistic crisis facing Europe.

**URL**: https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?brand=ucpress&chunk.id=d0e3993&docId=ft6q2nb3wh

**Assessment**: Widely regarded as one of the most profound interpretations of Nietzsche, though controversial for its relationship to Heidegger's Nazi period and for sometimes imposing Heidegger's own concerns onto Nietzsche's texts.

---

### C. Deleuze's Nietzsche Revival

#### Gilles Deleuze - Nietzsche and Philosophy (1962)

**Source**: Gilles Deleuze, *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (Columbia University Press, 2006), original French 1962.

**Credibility**: Seminal work that revitalized Nietzsche studies in France; foundational for poststructuralist reception.

**Summary**: Deleuze offers an affirmative reading emphasizing:

- Three crucial concepts: multiplicity, becoming, and affirmation
- Nietzsche as a systematic philosopher who overcomes Hegel
- The eternal return as "return of the different" not "return of the same"
- Active over reactive forces as the key dialectical distinction
- Nietzsche's critique as "symptomatology" revealing values

**Key Thesis**: Nietzsche challenged the entire tradition of Western philosophy by introducing sense and value into philosophical discourse.

**URL**: https://cup.columbia.edu/book/nietzsche-and-philosophy/9780231138772/

**Assessment**: Transformative for Nietzsche scholarship; opened new avenues for reading Nietzsche as affirmative rather than merely critical. Has been criticized from various quarters—Marxists see it as depoliticizing Nietzsche, while others argue it imposes Deleuze's categories onto Nietzsche.

---

### D. Karl Jaspers - Nietzsche as Existentialist

#### Karl Jaspers - Nietzsche: An Introduction to the Understanding of His Philosophical Activity (1936)

**Source**: Karl Jaspers, *Nietzsche: An Introduction to the Understanding of His Philosophical Activity* (University of California Press, 1991).

**Credibility**: Important early 20th-century interpretation by an existentialist philosopher; scholarly and respectful.

**Summary**: Jaspers reads Nietzsche as an existential-experimentalist whose philosophy:

- Explores limit-situations and human finitude
- Cannot be systematized into coherent doctrine
- Represents philosophical thinking as experimentation
- Was appropriated by Nazis despite being fundamentally opposed to their ideology

**Key Insight**: Nietzsche's "mighty solitude" was necessary for his philosophical task of revaluing values.

**URL**: https://melpomeneswork.com/review-of-nietzsche-an-introduction-to-the-understanding-of-his-philosophical-activity-karl-jaspers/

**Assessment**: Groundbreaking interpretation that distinguished Nietzsche from Nazi appropriation; influenced Walter Kaufmann's more accessible readings.

## II. Controversies and Rejections

### A. The Nazi Appropriation Controversy

**The Core Problem**: Nietzsche became associated with Nazism primarily through his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche and the Nietzsche Archive she controlled.

**Key Facts**:
- Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche married Bernhard Förster, an anti-Semite who founded an "Aryan colony" in Paraguay
- She served as Nietzsche's literary executor after his 1889 mental collapse
- She selectively edited, censored, and in some cases forged Nietzsche's works
- Hitler attended her funeral in 1935

**The Will to Power Fabrication**:
- Elisabeth constructed *The Will to Power* from Nietzsche's notebooks
- She selected fragments supportive of her own views
- She omitted or suppressed materials showing Nietzsche's anti-anti-Semitism and rejection of German nationalism
- The book Nietzsche would have recognized as his "magnum opus" was actually Elisabeth's creation

**Source**: Jillian Hess, "P.S. Notes Misused: the Case of Nietzsche and the Nazis," *Substack*, March 2025.

**URL**: https://jillianhess.substack.com/p/ps-notes-misused-the-case-of-nietzsche

---

### B. Academic Rejection: Max Nordau

**Source**: Max Nordau, *Degeneration* (1892), a major critique of Nietzsche in his era.

**Credibility**: Popular work of its time; now largely discredited for its pseudo-psychiatric approach.

**Summary**: Nordau, a physician and cultural critic, labeled Nietzsche's philosophy as symptomatic of mental degeneration, dismissing him as a "fool" whose ideas reflected pathological conditions rather than legitimate philosophy.

**Assessment**: While Nordau's methods are now considered flawed, his work illustrates the visceral rejection Nietzsche's ideas provoked in respectable intellectual circles.

---

### C. The Vienna Circle Reception

**Source**: Academic paper examining Vienna Circle's reception of Nietzsche.

**Summary**: Contrary to expectations, Moritz Schlick and other Vienna Circle members admired Nietzsche, praising him for anticipating Logical Empiricism and rejecting metaphysical systems. Schlick explicitly rejected the interpretation of Nietzsche as a militarist or proto-fascist.

**URL**: https://jhaponline.org/jhap/article/view/4276/3759

## III. Contemporary Reception During Nietzsche's Lifetime

### A. Early German Reception (1870s-1880s)

**Key Developments**:
- Initial academic dismissal of Nietzsche's philological work
- The Pernerstorfer Circle in Vienna adopted Nietzsche early
- Growing interest among young intellectuals seeking alternatives to positivism
- German soldiers received copies of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* as gifts during WWI

**Source**: Wikipedia, "Influence and reception of Friedrich Nietzsche."

**URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence_and_reception_of_Friedrich_Nietzsche

---

### B. The Dreyfus Affair

**Significance**: When French intellectuals defended Alfred Dreyfus against anti-Semitic charges, the anti-Semitic Right labeled them "Nietzscheans"—the first instance of "Nietzschean" as a term of abuse.

**Assessment**: This paradoxical acceptance by opposed camps remains characteristic of Nietzsche reception.

---

### C. Georg Brandes and Scandinavian Reception

**Source**: Correspondence and lectures by Georg Brandes, Danish literary critic.

**Summary**: Brandes was one of the first major intellectuals to engage seriously with Nietzsche, organizing lectures on him in the 1880s and helping establish his reputation in Scandinavia.

## IV. Nietzsche's Influence on Philosophers

### A. Existentialism

#### Jean-Paul Sartre
- Embraced Nietzsche's rejection of God and absolute values
- Developed atheistic existentialism partly in Nietzsche's spirit
- Though influenced by Nietzsche, Sartre emphasized human freedom more than Nietzsche's aristocratic radicalism

#### Albert Camus
-Explored the absurd in Nietzschean terms
- Affinity with Nietzsche's confrontation with nihilism
- Differed in his rejection of Nietzsche's "yes-saying" to life

**Source**: "Nietzsche's Influence on Existentialism and Postmodern Thought," Christian Leaders Institute.

**URL**: https://christianleaders.org/mod/page/view.php?id=101153&lang=ar

---

### B. Poststructuralism and Postmodernism

#### Michel Foucault
- Explicitly declared himself "Nietzschean"
- Adapted Nietzsche's genealogical method
- Developed power/knowledge analysis influenced by will to power
- Used Nietzsche to critique Enlightenment rationalism

#### Jacques Derrida
- Influenced by Nietzsche's critique of metaphysics
- Developed deconstruction partly in response to Nietzsche
- "Spurs: Nietzsche's Styles" is a key deconstructive reading

#### Gilles Deleuze
- See major critical works above
- Further developed Nietzsche's concepts of becoming and difference
- Created "Nietzsche and Philosophy" as foundation for his own philosophy

**Sources**:
1. Ron Brown, "The Influence of Nietzsche on Postmodern Thought" (SFU Summit).
2. Ashley Woodward, "Nietzscheanism and Poststructuralism" (Cambridge University Press).

### C. Critical Theory and Frankfurt School

#### Theodor Adorno
- Critically engaged with Nietzsche while rejecting his aristocratic politics
- Found value in Nietzsche's critique of Enlightenment rationality
- Differed fundamentally on questions of democracy and social justice

#### Max Horkheimer
- Engaged with Nietzsche's critique of reason
- Sought to appropriate critical insights while rejecting irrationalism

**Assessment**: The Frankfurt School represents a nuanced reception—appreciating Nietzsche's critique while firmly rejecting his politics.

---

### D. Later Anglophone Philosophy

#### Walter Kaufmann
- **Major Role**: Rebuilt Nietzsche's reputation in English-speaking world
- Published influential translations and interpretations
- Argued Nietzsche was not a Nazi or proto-fascist
- Distinguished between Nietzsche's actual views and Elisabeth's forgeries

#### Leo Strauss
- Influenced by Nietzsche's critique of modernity
- Developed "Straussian" approach to philosophy partly in response to Nietzsche
- Influenced American political philosophy significantly

## V. Nietzsche's Relations with Other Thinkers

### A. Arthur Schopenhauer

**Initial Influence**: Nietzsche discovered Schopenhauer's *The World as Will and Representation* in 1865 and experienced it as revelatory:

> "I don't know what demon whispered to me: 'Take this book home.' Back at home, I threw myself into the corner of a sofa with my new treasure, and began to let that dynamic, dismal genius work on me." — Nietzsche

**Key Influences from Schopenhauer**:
- Focus on the will as central to human existence
- Emphasis on suffering as fundamental to human condition
- Art as a means of escaping the suffering of existence
- Pessimism as a legitimate philosophical stance

**The Break**: Nietzsche eventually rejected Schopenhauer's pessimism and quietism, developing his own life-affirming philosophy:

**Reasons for Rejection**:
1. Schopenhauer's view that suffering is inextricable from existence
2. Advocacy of compassion as moral virtue
3. Denial of the will as a solution to suffering
4. Quietism and resignation as proper attitudes

**Nietzsche's Response**: Where Schopenhauer saw denial of the will, Nietzsche advocated affirmation of life; where Schopenhauer saw compassion as virtue, Nietzsche saw it as weakness; where Schopenhauer saw suffering as metaphysical, Nietzsche saw it as potentially eliminable.

**Sources**:
1. A. A. Adedire, "Why did Nietzsche break with Schopenhauer's Idealism?" *Essentia Foundation*, 2024.
2. Philosophy Matters, "Nietzsche's Response to Schopenhauer."

**URLs**:
- https://www.essentiafoundation.org/why-did-nietzsche-break-with-schopenhauers-idealism/reading/
- https://www.philosophymatters.net/single-post/nietzsche-s-response-to-schopenhauer

### B. Charles Darwin

**Complex Relationship**: Nietzsche's engagement with Darwin was constant and shaped his philosophy, though increasingly antagonistic.

**Initial Attraction**:
- Darwin represented a radical break from conventional morality
- Evolutionary theory suggested a naturalistic account of human origins
- The concept of species transformation challenged essentialist thinking

**Nietzsche's Criticisms of Darwin**:

1. **Survival of the Weak**: Nietzsche argued that Darwinian selection favors the mediocre, not the highest types
   > "The elimination of the strokes of luck, the selection in favor of those who have come off better, the progress of the species—the very opposite is quite palpably the case: the elimination of the strokes of excellence, the selection in favor of the below-average types."

2. **Overemphasis on External Constraints**: Darwin overestimated environmental determinism
   > "The error of the Darwinist school has become a problem for me: how can one be so blind as to miss the supreme innocence of becoming?"

3. **Denial of Will to Power**: Darwin reduced life to mere survival; Nietzsche saw creative power as the fundamental drive

4. **Rejection of Progress**: Nietzsche rejected the idea that evolution means improvement

**Sources**:
1. Dirk R. Johnson, *Nietzsche's Anti-Darwinism* (Cambridge University Press).
2. H. James Birx, "Nietzsche & Evolution," *Philosophy Now*.

**URLs**:
- https://assets.cambridge.org/97805211/96789/excerpt/9780521196789_excerpt.pdf
- https://philosophynow.org/issues/29/Nietzsche_and_Evolution

**Assessment**: Nietzsche accepted evolution as fact but rejected the moral and philosophical conclusions drawn from it by Darwinists.

### C. Richard Wagner

**The Friendship (1868-1876)**:
- Nietzsche met Wagner in 1868 and was immediately captivated
- Wagner represented the genius artist Nietzsche sought
- *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) was dedicated to Wagner
- Nietzsche saw Wagner as the embodiment of the new German spirit

**Shared Interests**:
- Schopenhauer's philosophy
- Greek tragedy (particularly the Dionysian)
- German cultural renewal
- Anti-rationalism in art

**The Break (1876-1888)**:
Nietzsche's break with Wagner was gradual but decisive. Key factors:

1. **Parsifal and Christianity**: Wagner's turn to Christian themes in his final opera disgusted Nietzsche
   > Nietzsche saw Parsifal as a betrayal of art for religious consolation

2. **Bayreuth Festival**: Nietzsche found the cult around Wagner insufferable
   > "I made the mistake of going to Bayreuth with an ideal in my breast, and was, therefore, doomed to suffer the most bitter disappointment."

3. **Wagner's Anti-Semitism and Nationalism**: These became increasingly pronounced
   > Nietzsche explicitly rejected Wagner's alignment with German nationalism and anti-Semitism

4. **The "Mortal Insult"**: A medical incident involving Wagner's interference with Nietzsche's health information caused lasting damage to the relationship

**After the Break**:
- *The Case of Wagner* (1888): Devastating polemic against Wagner
- *Nietzsche Contra Wagner*: Collection of earlier writings attacking Wagner
- Nietzsche never ceased acknowledging Wagner's musical genius while condemning his philosophy

**Sources**:
1. ThoughtCo, "Why Did Nietzsche Break With Wagner?"
2. Roger Scruton, "Nietzsche on Wagner."

**URLs**:
- https://www.thoughtco.com/why-did-nietzsche-break-with-wagner-2670457
- https://www.roger-scruton.com/articles/31-understanding-music/181-nietzsche-on-wagner

**Assessment**: The Nietzsche-Wagner relationship illustrates how Nietzsche could be both influenced by and deeply critical of those who shaped his early thought.

## VI. The Nazi Misuse of Nietzsche's Ideas

### A. Historical Overview

**The Misappropriation**: Following Nietzsche's death and especially during the Nazi period, his philosophy was systematically distorted to serve ideologies he would have opposed.

**Key Misrepresentations**:

1. **Will to Power**: Transformed from psychological/metaphysical concept to political program of domination
   - Nietzsche's actual concept was more nuanced—about creative self-overcoming, not mere domination

2. **Übermensch**: Reduced from individual self-creation to racial hierarchy
   - Nietzsche meant the overcoming of human limitations, not biological superiority

3. **Master/Slave Morality**: Twisted to justify racial supremacy
   - Nietzsche used these as psychological types, not biological categories

4. **Eternal Return**: Ignored in favor of more politically useful concepts

### B. Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's Role

**Source**: Carol Diethe, *Nietzsche's Sister and the Will to Power: A Biography of Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche*.

**Actions**:
- Married Bernhard Förster, prominent anti-Semite
- Founded the Nueva Germania colony in Paraguay for "Aryan" settlers
- Controlled Nietzsche Archive after his collapse
- Edited, censored, and in some cases forged Nietzsche's works
- Welcomed Hitler and Nazi officials to the Nietzsche Archive
- Sent birthday telegrams to Mussolini

**Quote from Nietzsche**: In *Ecce Homo*, Nietzsche wrote of his sister:
> "The treatment I've been receiving at the hands of my mother and sister fills me with unspeakable horror: here a veritable Hell-machine is at work."

### C. The Will to Power Problem

**The Fabrication**:
- Nietzsche never published *The Will to Power* as a book
- Elisabeth assembled it from his notebooks after his death
- She selected fragments supporting her views
- She omitted passages showing Nietzsche's opposition to anti-Semitism and nationalism
- The ordering and presentation distorted Nietzsche's actual philosophy

**Source**: David T. Moore, "Nietzsche's Influence on Hitler's Mein Kampf."

**URL**: https://marcuse.faculty.history.ucsb.edu/classes/133p/133p04papers/MKalishNietzNazi046.htm

### D. Academic Response and Rehabilitation

**The Rehabilitation**:
Starting in the 1950s, scholars began rescuing Nietzsche from Nazi appropriation:

**Key Figures**:
- Walter Kaufmann: Produced reliable translations and argued against Nazi interpretation
- Karl Schlechta: Published the "complete" notebooks revealing Elisabeth's manipulations
- Mazzino Montinari and Giorgio Colli: Produced the critical edition of Nietzsche's works

**Key Arguments for Rehabilitation**:
1. Nietzsche explicitly rejected anti-Semitism ("I call Christianity the one great curse")
2. Nietzsche criticized German nationalism and Bismarck
3. Nietzsche's concept of the Übermensch emphasized self-creation, not domination
4. The Will to Power was Elisabeth's construction, not Nietzsche's intended work

**Source**: Wikipedia, "Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche."

**URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabeth_F%C3%B6rster-Nietzsche

### E. Ongoing Debate

**The Nuanced Position**: Contemporary scholarship generally agrees that:
1. Nietzsche was not a Nazi or proto-fascist
2. His actual views were incompatible with Nazi ideology
3. His works were systematically distorted to serve Nazi purposes
4. However, certain aspects of his thought—anti-egalitarianism, critique of democracy, aristocratic radicalism—created intellectual resources that Nazis could exploit

**Remaining Controversies**:
- Was Nietzsche's thought inherently dangerous regardless of intent?
- Do his critiques of democracy and egalitarianism align with fascist politics?
- How should we judge the gap between Nietzsche's intentions and his work's effects?

## IX. Methodological Notes

**Distinguishing Primary from Secondary Sources**:

- **Primary Sources**: Nietzsche's own works and correspondence
- **Secondary Sources**: All interpretive works by others about Nietzsche

**Credibility Assessment Framework**:

1. **Academic Peer Review**: Published by university presses or in peer-reviewed journals
2. **Citation Frequency**: Extensively cited in scholarly literature
3. **Methodological Transparency**: Clear methodology and argument structure
4. **Source Criticism**: Engages with Nietzsche's actual texts, not just popular summaries
5. **Historical Context**: Situates Nietzsche within his historical context

**Limitations of This Research**:

1. Some sources cited are from secondary websites summarizing academic works
2. Full evaluation of scholarly debates requires access to specialized journals
3. The Nazi appropriation question remains politically charged and contested
4. Nietzsche interpretation continues to evolve
